The Billavas

The Billava, Billoru and Biruveru people are an ethnic Tuluva group of India. They make up about 18% of the population of coastal Karnataka. They are found traditionally in the Tulu Nadu region and are primarily engaged in toddy tapping, cultivation and other activities. They have used both missionary education and Sri Narayana Guru's reform movement to uplift themselves.

Etymology and origins

The Billavas trace their origin to Tulu Nadu, a complex linguistic environment. This compact region lies on the coastal belt of Karnataka and Kerala, with natural boundaries formed by the Arabian Sea, the hills of the Western Ghats and the rivers Suvarna and Chandragiri. It includes the South Canara district of Karnataka and the Kasaragod area of Kerala, which were once united for administration within the Madras Presidency.

Many languages and dialects are found there, including Tulu, Kannada, Konkani and Marathi. Kannada and Tulu are common throughout, and it is Tulu that gave the region its name.

Traditionally, Kannada is used in formal situations such as education, while Tulu is the lingua franca of everyday life. Tulu is more accepted as the primary language in the north of Tulu Nadu, while areas south of the Netravati river show a more traditional, though gradually fading, separation between the two languages.

A form known as Common Tulu has emerged as an accepted standard for formal communication. Of its four versions, shaped by geography and by the concentration of caste groups, Northern Common Tulu is gradually taking over the other three. As of 1998, the Brahmin community used Common Tulu only to speak with those outside their caste, while communities such as the Bunts, Billavas and Gouds used it frequently, and tribal communities were increasingly adopting it in place of their own dialects.

Worship of Bhutas

The Billavas were among the many communities excluded from the Hindu temples of Brahmins, and they traditionally worship spirits in a practice known as Bhuta Kola. S. D. L. Alagodi wrote in 2006 that among the Hindus of South Canara, a little over ten per cent are Brahmins, and the others, though nominally Hindus, are really worshippers of tutelary deities and bhutas.

The venues for Bhuta Kola are temple structures called Bhutasthana or Garidi, as well as numerous shrines. The officiators are a subcaste of Billavas known as Poojary (priest), and their practices are known as pooja. Families often keep a place in their home for a particular Bhuta, and worship there is called Bhuta Nema.

Iyer considered the twin heroes Koti and Chennayya to be the most prevalent of the Billava Bhutas. He described the spirits as belonging to people who in life had gained an unusual local reputation, whether for good or evil, or who had met a sudden or violent death. Beyond these are demons of the jungle, of the waste, of the village boundaries, and mischievous ones who throw stones on houses.

Tulu bhutas, though they have their vengeful aspects, are often benign, protective figures, ancestral spirits and heroes who have been assimilated to the ranks of minor deities. (Ghosh)

Ghosh draws a distinction between the Bhuta of southern India and the similarly named demons of the north, where the word generally means a ghost or a malign presence.

A shared tradition across society

Bhuta Kola is practised by a large section of Tulu Nadu society, from landlords to Dalits, and every level has its place in it. Those at the top provide patronage, communities such as the Billava officiate and tend the shrines, and those at the bottom enact the rituals, which share features with regional theatre such as Kathakali and Yakshagana. The pooja rituals include devil-dancing, performed by the Paravar or Naike, and the Bunts, who were historically ranked above the Billava, rely on the Poojary to officiate.

As major landowners, the Bunts held geographic hubs around which tenant farmers and workers were dispersed. The Billavas, among those dispersed people, were bound to their landlords by the need for a livelihood and spread out so that they could not easily unite. The Bhuta belief system also offered remedies for social and legal issues, giving a framework for day-to-day living.

Livelihood and heroes

In the nineteenth century the Billavas were frequently small tenant farmers and agricultural labourers working for Bunt landowners. Writing in 1930, Iyer described the community as mostly involved in toddy tapping, with some in agriculture and, in places, as peasant tenant landholders known as raiyats.

A 1961 report of the Indian Council of Agricultural Research noted that the Billavas are concentrated mostly in South Kanara district. Though toddy tappers by profession, they rely mostly on cultivation and are generally small landowners or lessees.

According to Ghosh, the Billavas are traditionally associated with the martial arts. The famous brothers Koti and Chennaya, the best-known pair of Tuluva heroes, are archetypal heroes of the community and symbolise the often hostile competition between the Billavas and the Bunts. Neither Thurston nor Iyer mentions this claim.

Narayana Guru and Billawa

Narayana Guru's influence on the Billawa community is profound. He challenged caste inequality and inspired communities across Kerala and coastal Karnataka. His work in education, temple reform and community organisation joined inner growth with practical social change.

His guidance became part of the Billawa community's story of dignity, organisation, education and shared progress. His emphasis on education, unity and self-respect offered a practical path for communities seeking greater dignity and opportunity. These principles shaped Billawa institutions, associations and service activities.

For Billawa families today, remembering Narayana Guru means carrying forward a tradition of cultural pride, mutual support, learning and responsibility to the wider community.
